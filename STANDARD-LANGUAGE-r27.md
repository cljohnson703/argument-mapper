# Standard logic language coverage

The current package uses classical inference rules plus the map's rules for objections, rebuttals and failure to establish a premise. The displayed assessments are Warranted and Unwarranted, relative to the argument as presented. Internal unresolved states do not change the underlying classical truth tables. Separate modal, higher-order, relevant and paraconsistent packages are future work.

### October 5, last: one Google account on two devices (r27.77)

The user: "the display-name lease refresh in `diffAndStamp` drops the verified Google uid from the signed-in user's claim, which defeats the 'same verified person on two devices' exemption in `applySignIn`."

### October 5, later: a link read as its label is one name (r27.72)

The user, on r27.71's "-O1" read as "not O1": "We need to fix that. Any suggestions on how? For what it's worth, if it's a linked premise like -O1 and someone wants to negate it, the negation would be outside of the syntax for the linked -O1."

* **One name.** The deductive check reads a premise link as its box's words, and a link to a box that is gone, or back round a loop, as the box's label. That label is now one name however it is spelled: a link to weak objection -O1 is -O1, not "not O1". A negation goes outside the link -- "It is not the case that [-O1]", or "~[-O1]" in symbols. Typed without a link, "-O1" is still "not O1".
* **How.** A WORD JOINER (U+2060), the character that says "these are one word", goes before each hyphen of the label as the check reads it. The English reader already kept "-O1" one word in a sentence; the symbolic reader read a hyphen at the start of a word as "not", and one inside a word ("M1S1-O1") as "not" between two letters. It now reads a joined name as one sentence letter, spelled as the English reader spells it, so "[-O1] -> S4" and "[-O1]" give modus ponens. The joiner is invisible where the check shows the words, and never goes into a box.
* **Derive Parent** writes such a name back as its link. From "If it rains, then [-O1]" and "It rains", the new box says [-O1] (struck through while its box is gone), where it said "-O1", which reads as "not O1": the opposite of what was derived.
* **What changes.** With a deleted weak objection's link, "If S4, then O1" and "[-O1]" were certified by modus tollens; now the step is not recognized. A complex name in a formula ("[M1S1a-O1] -> S4" with "[M1S1a-O1]") was not recognized; now it is modus ponens. Labels without a hyphen read exactly as before.

### September 29, later: the syntax tree, stage one -- parts of speech and phrases (r27.59)

* **What it is** (the user: "Maybe we need an internal syntax tree so the checker can disambiguate a little more easily, especially by telling different syntactical elements apart"; "Let's proceed to stage one of the syntax tree").

    * a name, or a pronoun;
    * a determiner or quantifier with adjectives and nouns ("the black raven", "the Senate", "the rich");

    * two joined ("Alice and Bob", "both the raven and the crow");
    * with a relative clause ("the dog that barked"; "the match, which was scheduled for noon,").
  * **Verb groups:** helping verbs, adverbs, and a verb.
  * **Noun or verb.** Where a word could be either, the phrase decides:
    * agreement: "the dog bites", "ravens fly";
    * a sure verb over a possible one: a helping verb, a past, a verb the lists know;

'If she has this property, then it follows that she won't be surprised', 'She is surprised', so, 'She doesn't have that property.'" So "If ..., then she would not be surprised" has "she is not surprised" for its consequent, and modus tollens with "She is surprised" certifies; modus ponens gives the plain claim. The if-clause is read back from the past the "would" puts it in: "if she knew everything" is the present ("if she knew everything now"), "if she had known everything" the past ("she knew everything"), "if she were rich" "she is rich"; "would have been surprised" is "was surprised". Where the past tense may mean the past, that reading is offered ("if Mary had known everything, ..."), and named in the diagnosis where it is the one that gives the step. Said alone, "She would not be surprised" keeps its "would": it is not "She is not surprised". Counterfactuals still do not chain, transpose, export or turn material. With "should" in its place ("then she should not be surprised") the step is not certified -- "should" hedges ("presumably") or says what ought to be, and what is so refutes neither -- and the diagnosis says that with "would" it would follow.
* **"When" that says when lets a "not" through.** "Mary is not surprised when she leaves the room" is the denial of "Mary is surprised when she leaves the room": a "when" that names one time is transparent to the "not" in its clause. And "She does not learn X", said without the time, denies "When E, she learns X": what is so at no time is not so then. The note that "when" may mean "if" or "whenever" stays, and on that reading the two are no denials.
* **The Mary's Room steps pass in their own words.** "If while in the room, Mary has acquired all the physical facts ..., then she would not be surprised when she leaves the room" with "Mary is surprised when she leaves the room", against S5: modus tollens. "If Mary acquires knowledge-how when she exits the room and sees a ripe red tomato, then she does not learn a new fact about the sensation of seeing red, namely its subjective character" with its antecedent, against S6: modus ponens. S5, S6, and "If, while in the room, ..., and when Mary exits the room and sees a ripe red tomato, she learns ..., then there are non-physical facts about color sensations": modus ponens with the conditions together, without an extra "if". The fixes: a verb in -s with "a" or "the" after it continues the phrase before its "and" ("... when she exits the room and sees a ripe red tomato"), and after a "when" clause it stays in that clause; a name and a verb make a clause at any length; "When P, Q" is a clause, so "if A, and when B, C, then D" has two conditions; a front frame ("while in the room,") does not reach past a later part with a frame of its own; and a pronoun in a predicate with a noun phrase before it in its clause ("the sensation of seeing red, namely its subjective character") is matched as written. A pronoun with nothing before it ("Mary eats it"; "she" in a box that names nobody) is still asked about.
* **Parentheses in the chooser (a note on r27.45).** *(Superseded in r27.48: the readings below now use the that-clause convention, not brackets.)* Readings use "both ... and", "either ... or" and "neither ... nor" as English's own brackets. Brackets remain only where English has no grouping word: around an "iff" ("Bob sings iff (Mary dances iff Ann runs)"); for how far "necessarily" reaches ("Necessarily, (Bob sings iff Mary dances)" against "(Necessarily, Bob sings) iff Mary dances"); and for how far a belief reaches ("Bob believes that (Mary sings iff Ann dances)" against "(Bob believes that Mary sings) iff Ann dances").
* **The Evaluation Overview.** "Go to" and "Delete" are gone from its entries: clicking a note (not the text entry box) shows its box on the map, as "Go to" did; a note is deleted by the trash can beside reply and edit, which replaces the X.
* **A box moved among its co-premises takes its branch.** Children with no target of their own stand on the first box; the reorder had left them at the first slot, so the co-premise moved there inherited them. Every child now goes with its box.

### September 24, evening: grouping words, conditions together, "given that", letters as shorthand (r27.45)

* **"Both … and", "either … or" and "neither … nor" group, as brackets do.** Each opens a group that holds its own "and", "or" or "nor", and runs on to a comma with a connective after it, or to the end; a group inside another's first part ends at the other's own word. So "Bob sings, and both Mary dances and Ann runs" is Bob sings and (Mary dances and Ann runs) -- it had read "both Mary dances" as a claim; "Either Bob sings or Mary dances, and Ann runs" is (A or B) and C -- it had been A or (B and C); "Neither Bob sings nor Mary dances, and Ann runs" keeps Ann out of the "neither"; "Bob sings and either Mary dances or Ann runs" and "Tom walks if both Bob sings and Mary dances" are no longer flagged, the words having settled them; "Bob loves both Mary and Ann, and Tom sings" is two claims, not one long verb phrase.
* **The chooser's readings use them.** Where two plain claims are grouped, a reading says "both A and B" or "either A or B"; a list, or a claim set apart from the rest, is numbered -- "(1) If Bob sings, then both Mary dances and Ann runs, and (2) Tom walks" -- with (i), (ii) inside a numbered list; brackets stay only where English has no grouping word (around an "iff", or the reach of "necessarily" or of a belief). A "not" in a verb phrase is offered as whole sentences, "Poe is not either black or white" and "Poe is either not black or white" (it had been two fragments, which could not be written into the box). "It is not the case that P if Q" offers "It is not the case that if Q, then P". Every reading can still be written into the box and reads back as itself.
* **Conditions together, given one by one.** "If Q and imagination is a non-physical event, then R", with "Q" and "Imagination is a non-physical event" as premises, was not certified: the reader takes "a non-physical event" as two claims (not physical; an event), so the condition had three conjuncts and the premises two. Modus ponens with the conditions together now takes a premise that is itself a conjunction as a run of the conjuncts, in its own order, as conjunction introduction already did. In another order ("small and black" for "black and small") it is still not certified.
* **Condition lists.** "If A and B and C, then D", "If A, and if B, and if C, then D" and "If A, B, and C, then D" now all certify with A, B and C given. The first and the last failed when a clause had a slip of agreement ("Conscious mental occurrences causes physical effects E"), which did not look like a clause and was glued to the one before it; a plural subject of up to four words and a verb is now a clause, "of" in the subject still making a noun phrase ("a series of lights"). The second was read as two conditionals side by side, the first ending "…, and if B": a consequent does not begin with "and", "or" or "if". Clauses in a comma list are a clause too, so "Mary dances, Ann runs, Tom walks, and Sue swims" is four claims (with four it had been one predication of Mary).
* **"Given that" is flagged, and asks.** It may give a reason, as "because" does, or a condition, as "if" does, and the words do not always say which: the box offers "Because P, Q" (which the inference-word flag then asks to split into two boxes) and "If P, then Q"; after its claim, "Q because P" and "Q if P".
* **A letter after a plural is a shorthand.** "Physical effects E are fully caused by purely physical events" had been about one thing named "physical effects E", so with "All physical effects are fully caused by purely physical events" and "Physical effects E is a physical effect" -- a premise as empty as "x = x" -- it was certified by universal modus ponens, though the universal only restates it. A capital letter after a plural noun (at the start, or after a determiner or a known adjective) is now read as a shorthand for the phrase, and the box says so: the claim is about physical effects. From the universal alone the step is flagged -- read with "all", it is the same claim. "Bob sees E", "The mind causes E" and "the students A and B" keep their letters; "the ravens" is read as before.
* **The checker's own words agree in number.** A case it asks for or supplies of a plural term reads "The ravens are ravens", not "The ravens is a raven"; and a reading's words take the box's capitals for names only ("all physical effects", not "all Physical effects", where the box began "Physical effects ...").
* **The Logic panel folds from a button that looks like one** -- the chevron beside "Logic" in the Evaluation Overview, now outlined, with a tooltip saying "Collapse the Logic panel" or "Expand the Logic panel". The panel remembers which it was.

### September 24, later: inference words flagged, numbered lists (r27.44)

* **A box with an inference word is flagged, and no step with it is certified.** A box holds one claim: its reasons are the boxes below it, what it is a reason for is the box above. So "because", "since", "therefore", "thus", "hence", "so" and their like either put a parent and a child in one node or make a premise sound established. The step's tag names the word -- "✗ “because” in a box" -- and says what to do:
  * *A reason inside the box* -- "because", "since", "seeing that", "inasmuch as", "on the grounds that", "the reason is that", "as a result of", "∵": "put “it rains” in a box of its own under “The ground is wet”".
  * *A whole inference* -- a conclusion word between two clauses ("It rains, so the ground is wet"; "It rains; therefore the ground is wet"; "P ∴ Q"): "put “It rains” in a box of its own under “the ground is wet”".
  * *A claim that says it was inferred* -- "Therefore, the soul is immortal", "The soul is therefore immortal", "It follows that ...", "In conclusion, ...", "As a result, ...", "Hence ...": what it follows from is the boxes below it, so the word says nothing the check can use, and in a premise it makes the claim sound established: leave it out. (The reader had silently dropped a leading "therefore", "so" or "it follows that", and read "in conclusion" as a word that adds nothing.)
  * Not flagged: words inside quotation marks, which are mentioned ('the argument "P because Q" is invalid'); "since" of time ("since 1990", "ever since", "since the war"); "so long as", "so that", "so is Fido", "so far", "if so"; "thus far"; "accordingly" of manner ("Bob acted accordingly"); and phrases that do not begin a clause -- "it is not warranted to conclude that P", "there is no reason to conclude that P" are weak objections, not conclusions. "Given that" keeps its own flag, which offers the reading "if".
  * Such a box gets no "✓ AXIOM" tag, and Derive Parent derives nothing from it; it says why.
* **Lists of three or more after "then" and "iff" stay flagged, and the user chooses** -- now among three readings, each written as a numbered list: the whole list ("If P, then (1) A, (2) B, and (3) C"), a list whose first item is the conditional ("(1) If P, then A, (2) B, and (3) C"), or all but the last ("(1) If P, then (A and B) and (2) C"). "S knows that p iff S believes that p, p is true, and S is justified" asks two things at once -- whether "knows that" takes in the "iff", and which list follows it -- and one choice among four readings now settles both.
* **Numbered lists.** Numbering says where a list begins: "(1) A, (2) B, and (3) C", "(i) ...", "(a) ...", "1) ...". The list is one part of the sentence at the place its numbering begins, and each item is one claim, read on its own (so "(1) S believes that p" does not take in "(2) p is true"). A list after "if" ends at its "then": "If (1) A, (2) B, and (3) C, then D". The numbering must start at 1, i or a and go on in order, each item must be a clause, and "and" and "or" may not be mixed. The chooser writes its numbered readings into the box in the same form.
* **Numbers in brackets that make no list refer to other claims**: "(1) and (2) entail (3)", "Premise (1) is false" are read as one claim, and say so. The first had been read as the claims "1" and "(2) entail (3)", and gave the second on its own.
* **Bracketed claims joined are read as a junction first** ("(S believes that p), (p is true), and (S is justified)"): the belief reader had taken the whole as one belief. And "(A), (B)" -- bracketed claims in a list -- counts as a clause, as "A, B" does.

### September 24: "iff" with its scope, brackets that group, very long boxes (r27.43)

* **"If and only if" is read with its scope.** The reader had split a sentence at its first "iff", wherever it stood, and read the rest as it came. It now finds the connective at the top of the sentence only, and where the sentence has another connective too, the words settle which takes in which or the box is flagged with each reading:
  * *Settled by the words.* "P iff Q, and Q iff R" is two biconditionals -- it had been read "P iff ((Q and Q) iff R)", which with "not Q" and "not R" certified "P". "If P iff Q, then R" has the biconditional as its condition (it had been two nonsense claims); "If and only if P, Q" is "Q if and only if P"; "(P iff Q) iff R" groups as bracketed. "P iff Q and R" keeps "Q and R" together, as "if P, then Q and R" does.
  * *Flagged, each reading offered.* A run -- "P iff Q iff R" says that each is equivalent to the next (as in mathematics), or groups them, "P iff (Q iff R)", and the two say different things; "P iff Q and R iff S" may also be two. A condition in front with the "iff" after it ("if P, then Q iff R"). "Necessarily", "probably", "according to Bob", "it has not been shown that" or somebody's belief in front ("Bob believes that P iff Q"): the whole biconditional, or its first side. An "and" or "or" after a comma ("P iff Q, and R"), as a conditional's is. An "if" or "unless" on either side ("P iff Q if R").
  * *Not flagged:* a denial in front. "It is not the case that P iff Q" may deny the whole or its first side, but not-(P iff Q) is (not-P) iff Q, so either reading says the same; it keeps its old one.
  * So "S knows that p iff S believes that p, p is true, and S is justified" is now flagged -- whether "knows that" takes in the "iff" is a question the words leave open -- and the chooser writes it "(S knows that p) iff S believes that p, p is true, and S is justified".
* **Brackets group in English as they do in symbols.** The reader found connectives in a copy of the sentence with its brackets blanked to spaces, and its searches took those spaces as part of the gap around a connective. So "if Bob sings, then (Mary dances and Ann runs)" lost its consequent and was not read; "(P or Q) and R", "either (P and Q) or R" and the chooser's own "(if P, then Q), and R" were not read; "(Bob sings) and (Mary dances)" was a claim about "(bob". Several readers also matched their connective in the raw words, so "(P) and (God exists if and only if God exists)" was cut at the "only if" inside the bracket. The blank is now a character no search takes for a space, every connective is found at the top level, and a bracketed part is one clause. A bracketed fuzzer (random "and", "or", "not", "if", "iff" over English clauses, every compound part bracketed) reads 20,000 sentences as they say, and of 20,000 steps shaped like rules and fallacies, with brackets inside, certifies 12,122 -- every rule instance -- and nothing invalid. Quoted clauses joined by connectives ("“Bob sings” or “Mary dances”") are read as the clauses joined.
* **Conditionals side by side, however many.** "If it rains, then the ground is wet, and if it snows, then the roads are icy, and if it hails, then the crops fail" had been read with "it snows then the roads" as a subject: two conditionals were taken apart, three were not.
* **"Because" and "since" anywhere make the whole sentence one claim.** "P iff Q because R" and "if P, then Q because R" had been split first, leaving only the part with "because" unread -- under a note that said the sentence was not read. "So is Fido" is still a clause cut short, not an inference. (Since r27.44 such a box is also flagged, and no step with it is certified: see above.)
* **A scope flag stays in its clause.** "Poe is not white and each raven is white" was flagged as a "not ... each" ambiguity -- the "not" of one conjunct with the "each" of the other -- and "it is not the case that each owl is white" as one too, though a sentential denial is not ambiguous. And after a relative clause, "or everything is an owl" is a claim of its own ("there is a crow that is not black or everything is an owl"), as "or Bob sings" was; "a crow that the owl saw or the bird heard" still continues the "that".
* **The dilemmas give their literal instances.** "Either the ground is wet or the ground is wet" from "it rains or it snows" and a conditional from each to "the ground is wet" is constructive dilemma (proof by cases also gives "the ground is wet"); "either it does not rain or it does not rain" is destructive dilemma where both conditionals begin "if it rains".
* **Every reading the chooser offers can be written into the box.** Its words had kept the reader's mark on a name ("There is a raven that Poe does not love" was offered with an invisible mark before "Poe"), and "(none)" after "does not love any raven"; bracketed readings of "necessarily" and "believes that" did not read back as themselves. All the flagged constructions now round-trip.
* **Very long boxes.** A box too long or involved to take apart -- more than 400 parts, or more than 600 part-readings to try -- is read as one claim, matched as written, and says so: "this box is too long for the check to take apart ... split it into boxes to check its steps". Within one sentence each part is read once (the reader had re-read the same words by one route and then another: twelve conditionals took 15,627 readings, and a run of thirty-two "iff"s filled the page's memory). A step whose boxes run past 1,500 characters gets no near-miss hints, which re-run the check under looser matching; certification is unchanged. Realistic sentences take one to forty part-readings, and a thousand-character paragraph about fifty milliseconds.

### September 22: the basis and its extensions, one rule per step, scope flagged, misreadings fixed

* **The basis is Hilbert's** (September 22). Eight axiom schemas, every universal closure of them, and one rule:

  | | schema | | schema |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | H1 | A → (B → A) | Q1 | ∀x A → A[t/x] |
  | H2 | (A → (B → C)) → ((A → B) → (A → C)) | Q2 | ∀x (A → B) → (∀x A → ∀x B) |
  | H3 | ¬¬A → A | Q3 | A → ∀x A, x not free in A |
  | E1 | t = t | E2 | t = s → (A → A′), A atomic |

  The other connectives are abbreviations, and a map writes them as themselves, so what they abbreviate is an axiom too, both ways round: "¬A" is "A → ⊥", "A ∧ B" is "¬(A → ¬B)", "A ∨ B" is "¬A → B", "A ↔ B" is "(A → B) ∧ (B → A)", "∃x A" is "¬∀x ¬A". **The one rule is modus ponens.**

  Every rule the check offers -- conjunction introduction and elimination, the dilemma, reductio, hypothetical syllogism, exportation, universal elimination, existential introduction and elimination, the identity rules -- is a step in the **Intro and elimination** group, on by default, and each is derived from the axioms: Kleene's ten axioms, `⊥ → A`, `A → A`, both ↔ rules, `A(t) → ∃x A`, and identity's symmetry, transitivity and compound substitution, 5,625 lines in all, every line checked in the app (`logic-r27-hilbert-test.js`). A supposition is discharged by the deduction theorem, which H1 and H2 give; a map still holds none.

  **Rule instances**, on by default, keep the uncurried conditionals a map is used to: "(A ∧ ¬A) → ⊥" states Contradiction's step, "((A → B) ∧ A) → B" modus ponens'. The axioms themselves are curried, as Hilbert's are -- that is what lets modus ponens be the only rule -- and a box that matches one is tagged "✓ AXIOM".
* **Pronouns** (September 23, a reported bug; r27.34 for the rest). "When Mary exits the room and sees a ripe red tomato, she learns a new fact about the sensation of seeing red" was flagged ambiguous for "she", which held back every step it appeared in. Two things changed:
  * **"he", "she", "him", "his" and "her" take the one name their own claim gives.** "Poe wrote a poem and his raven is black" is about Poe's raven; "Mary studied color and her knowledge is complete" about Mary's knowledge. Only that claim counts -- a name in another box never supplies a referent -- and the name must come first. With two names ("Mary told Alice she was late") the pronoun is left as written and flagged, as before. Where the pronoun is the object of the name's own clause it is somebody else -- English would say "herself" there -- so "Bob praised her" is left as written and flagged; across a clause boundary the two may be one, as in "Mary left because the dog bit her". "Mary's dog barked and she ran" names Mary, not "Mary's", and "it bit her again" reads "her" as the one bitten, since an adverb is not something owned. Two genders of pronoun are two people, and a claim that gives one name cannot say which of them it names: "Mary said he loves her" is left as written, while "He left and Mary arrived and she smiled" still reads "she". An object pronoun is measured from the last reading of the name, not only from the name: "Mary left and then she hurt her" leaves "her" alone, since the one who hurt is Mary already, while "Mary is a doctor and her patients trust her" reads both, a possessive being no such reading. And a pronoun may come first where the claim opens with a clause that waits for its main one -- "Before she left, Mary called", "When she exits the room, Mary learns a new fact" -- though nowhere else: "She met Mary" is still left as written.

  **Not inside what somebody believes, says or knows** (r27.38). "Mary believes that she is late" is no longer read as "Mary believes that Mary is late": the "she" may be Mary as she thinks of herself, and she can believe the one without the other -- Castañeda's "she*", Perry's shopper with the torn sack. In a report of what was said, the pronoun may not be Mary at all. So a pronoun after "believes", "thinks", "says", "hopes", "doubts" and the like, or after "knows that", "learns that", "sees that", "told Bob that", is kept as written, and the clause is matched as written, like any clause the reader keeps whole -- which also means the box is no longer flagged for it ("Bob believes that it rains" used to be). A pronoun outside such a clause still reads: "When Mary exits the room, she learns a new fact" is about Mary.

  **Why a pronoun is read at all.** Gender is never consulted, which is what makes this safe where names do not follow it: a gender-neutral name, or a name used outside its usual gender, is read exactly as any other. What the reading rests on is the node: a pronoun must find its referent among the words of its own box, there must be exactly one name to find, and the pronouns that look for it must want the same person. Where any of that fails -- no name, two names, two genders, or a place where English would have said "herself" -- nothing is read and the box is flagged as before. And the reading is never silent: the box says which name was used, so a wrong one can be seen and the name written out instead. The reading says what it did: *"she" is read as "Mary", the one name this claim gives*, so a wrong guess is visible. So "Mary sings and she dances" gives "Mary dances" by conjunction elimination, and a premise saying "she" matches a condition saying "Mary".
  * **A pronoun inside a claim the reader leaves whole no longer flags it.** Nothing looks inside such a claim -- it is matched as written -- so the pronoun settles nothing. A pronoun the reading does depend on, the subject of a predication ("She is happy") or an object in one, is still flagged. "They" is left alone throughout: the check's own wordings use it ("given A and B, they do not establish C").
* **Pairs of negations inside a rule instance** (September 23). While double-negation replacement is on, a conditional that states a rule's step up to pairs of negations states that step: "¬¬(P ∧ Q) → P" and "(P ∧ Q) → ¬¬P" are conjunction elimination's, "((A → B) ∧ ¬¬A) → B" modus ponens'. The explanation says a pair was taken out or put in. Switched off, the conditional must state the step exactly. A restatement ("P → P") is still no instance, and order is still content: "¬(P ∨ ¬P) → (P ∧ ¬P)" is a commutation beyond De Morgan's.
* **Names with capitals inside them** (r27.42). "H2O", "DNA", "McDonald" and "Au" are names on the right of "is" as elsewhere, so "Water is H2O" is an identity: with "Water is wet" it gives "H2O is wet" -- though still not "Necessarily, H2O is wet" from "Necessarily, water is wet". The note names each side as the box writes it ("H2O", "Dr. Jekyll"). A capitalized name with "the" in front ("the Evening Star") is still a description, as the rule for descriptions says.
* **Derive Parent reaches what the check certifies** (r27.41). It now takes a case spread over several premises ("every raven that sings is black", "Fido is a raven", "Fido sings" give "Fido is black" -- before, only a case in one box), the quantifier-bound "it", one alternative of a kind, and substitution of equivalents ("Bob runs if and only if Mary runs" and "Bob runs and Ann sings" give "Mary runs and Ann sings"). It still derives nothing from an ambiguous box.
* **Names of more than one word** (r27.40). "Clark Kent", "Mark Twain", "Samuel Clemens" and "John Stuart Mill" are each one name: as the subject of a verb ("Clark Kent wears glasses" was not read at all), on either side of an identity ("Clark Kent is Superman", "Superman is Clark Kent", "Mark Twain is the same person as Samuel Clemens"), in either place of a relation ("Lois loves Clark Kent" gives "Lois loves Superman"), and for a pronoun to take ("Clark Kent is tired and he is late"). So "Clark Kent is Superman" and "Clark Kent wears glasses" give "Superman wears glasses", while "Lois believes that Clark Kent is a reporter" still gives nothing of Superman. A part of a name is not the name ("Kent wears glasses" gives nothing), a common word before a name is no part of it ("Today Mary sings"), and the last word of a name keeps its letters -- "Clemens" had been read as the plural of "clemen", and in "Samuel Clemens wrote a novel" as a verb. A capitalized plural of a kind of people is still a plural ("All Greeks are mortal"). A title or an initial with a full stop is part of the name too (r27.41): "Dr. Jekyll is Mr. Hyde" and "Dr. Jekyll is a doctor" give "Mr. Hyde is a doctor"; "J. S. Mill", "St. Augustine", "Martin Luther King Jr." are each one name, and a pronoun takes the whole of one. The full stop ends no sentence there (Dr., Mr., Mrs., Ms., St., Prof., Rev., Jr., Sr. and the like, and a single capital), and it is kept in the words the checker writes back. A step that uses part of a name an identity gives whole asks about it -- *Did you mean ‘Dr. Jekyll’ where it says ‘Jekyll’? The identity is about Dr. Jekyll; if Jekyll is someone else, it says nothing about them.* -- since "Jekyll" may be another Jekyll.
* **A universal short of its case says what is missing** (r27.39). "Every raven is black" and nothing else, with "Fido is black" to show: the step explains *"Every raven is black" would give this once it is shown that ‘Fido is a raven’*. So with "Every raven is black if it sings" and "Fido is a raven" (‘Fido sings’ is missing), and with a kind whose alternatives a premise does not supply (‘Tweety is black or white’).
* **"Only Bob runs" and "even if" are flagged, with their readings** (r27.39, asked for). Each has two readings in a live dispute, and the box is now marked ambiguous and says what they are:
  * "Only Bob runs": *everything that runs is Bob*, or *Bob runs, and everything that runs is Bob* -- whether "only" asserts, presupposes or merely implicates its prejacent is Horn's question;
  * "Even if Mary runs, Bob runs" (or "Bob runs even if Mary runs"): *Bob runs, and if Mary runs, then Bob runs* -- the concessive reading most accounts give -- or *if Mary runs, then Bob runs*.

  A step that needs one of the readings says so: *Read as "Bob runs, and everything that runs is Bob", the step follows by conjunction elimination.* Until a reading is chosen the box derives nothing.

  **Choosing a reading** (r27.39, asked for). Click the step's **? AMBIGUOUS** tag: a small panel names the box and what is ambiguous in it, lists its readings written out as sentences -- the one the step needs says *the step follows by ...* -- and **Rewrite the box this way** replaces the box's words with the reading you pick, so the map says what it means; undo puts the old words back. It works for every ambiguity the checker raises, not only these two ("All ravens are not white" offers *No ravens are white* and *Not all ravens are white*). A reading the checker would not read back as itself cannot be written in; the panel says to put it in your own words. In review mode, or on a map shared read-only, the readings are shown but nothing is written. **Every step...** at the bottom opens the list of steps, which is what the tag used to do.

  To make the concessive reading writable, "Bob runs, and if Mary runs, then Bob runs" is now read as a claim and a conditional joined -- before, the conditional was taken as the end of the first claim's verb phrase.
* **"Anyone" in the object's place, and "everyone who ..."** (r27.38). "Bob loves anyone" and "Mary is a person" give "Bob loves Mary"; so do "Bob loves everyone who sings", "Mary is a person" and "Mary sings", and "Bob loves everything that sings" with "Fido sings". Before, only "every raven" and "everyone" were read there -- in the subject's place all of these already worked. "Anyone" is "everyone" only in a plain clause: after a denial ("does not love anyone") it is "someone", read as before, and in a condition ("if Bob loves anyone") it is not taken as universal. No existential import: "Bob loves anyone" does not give "Bob loves someone". And a quantifier word is no longer treated as a name when a sentence is put into symbols ("Bob loves anyone" had been read as a relation between Bob and something called "anyone"; nothing unsound followed from it, but the reading was wrong).
* **"Just in case" says so** (r27.38). "P just in case Q" is read as "P if and only if Q", its sense in philosophy, and the box now says so -- in everyday English it is a precaution ("bring an umbrella just in case it rains"), which says no such thing.
* **A description says so** (r27.37, asked for). "Ada is the winner", "Hesperus is the evening star", "Water is the liquid that fills the oceans": the box now says *"the winner" is a description, not a second name: this says what Ada is, so nothing said of Ada is carried over to it, or back*. Nothing about the checking changed -- a description never was a term here, and the identity rules never reached it -- but the silence was easy to mistake for an oversight. "Ada is the F" is covered too, where the reader keeps only the noun.

  Why it stays that way: a description is not a singular term. On Russell's reading "a = ιxF(x)" says that exactly one thing is F and a is it, so swapping one for the other is not identity's business but a definitional expansion -- and the expansion has a scope, which the words of one box do not settle. Nor is this only a matter of opaque contexts: since a description may fail to denote, even a denial divides the readings ("the king of France is not bald" is true if the quantifier goes inside the denial and false if it goes outside). A map that wants the Russellian conclusion can write the expansion out, and the basis takes it from there with ordinary identity substitution -- in a few boxes rather than one, which puts the scope decision where a reader can object to it. An ι shorthand, if it is ever wanted, belongs with the modal packages, as an expansion with an explicit scope marker rather than as a substitution rule.

  Identity locutions are untouched: "Ada is the same person as Bea" is still an identity, and "Poe is Edgar" still reads as one.
* **A pronoun the quantifier binds** (r27.36, asked for). "Every raven is black if it sings" and "If a raven sings, it is black" both say what "every raven that sings is black" says, and the checker now reads them that way: with "Fido is a raven" and "Fido sings" they give "Fido is black" by universal modus ponens. So do "everything is black if it is a raven", "no raven is black if it sings" and "every bird is happy if it sings or dances". Before, the pronoun had no referent the checker would accept and the box took part in nothing. The reading is shown: *"it" is read as what the quantifier speaks of: this says what "every raven that sings is black" says*.

  Three things it is not. The "it" of "it rains", "it is cold", "it seems that ..." stands for nothing, and those are left as conditionals -- the same list that already guarded "if something is a raven, then it is black". "Some raven is black if it sings" says something weaker than a universal, so it is not read this way; the determiner must be one that speaks of each such thing ("every", "each", "all", "any", "no", "everything", and in a condition "a", "an", "any", "something"). And a whole claim in the condition binds nothing: "if every raven sings, it is black" is left alone.
* **A restrictor that offers alternatives** (r27.36, asked for). "Every bird that is black or white is happy", with "Tweety is a bird" and "Tweety is black or white", gives "Tweety is happy" -- the relative clause's "or" is now read of an adjective as it always was of a verb ("that sings or dances"). One alternative on its own is enough (r27.39, asked for): "Every bird that is black or white is happy", "Tweety is a bird" and "Tweety is black" give "Tweety is happy" in one step. That is the same allowance the rule has always made for a list -- "every bird that sings and dances" is shown of Tweety by "Tweety sings" and "Tweety dances" in separate boxes -- made for alternatives: a part of the kind that offers alternatives is shown by any one of them. No switch is needed, since nothing about it depends on order or on anything contested. An alternative the kind does not offer shows nothing ("Tweety is red"). Both words at once leaves the grouping open ("that is black and old or white") and is not read.
* **Substitution of equivalents** (r27.36, asked for; a familiar step, on by default). A biconditional lets either side stand for the other inside another claim: "P if and only if Q" and "P and R" give "Q and R", and "R only if P" gives "R only if Q". It reaches only what the words lay open, which is what keeps Lois out of trouble: "Lois thinks that Clark is not Superman" is a claim the checker keeps whole, so nothing is put into it, and she is not made to think that Superman is not Superman. Nor is a counterfactual's supposition rewritten, where a claim with the same truth value is not the same supposition.

  For the same reason a name no longer goes into a modal predicate: "Ada might be tall", "must be tall", "could have been tall", "would be tall" keep the name they were given, as "is necessarily tall" already did. Names are rigid, so Kripke would allow the substitution; the checker refuses it because English "might" is also read of how things are said ("the teacher of Alexander might not have been the teacher of Alexander"), and because it cannot tell which reading a box intends. Say the word if you would rather have the Kripkean rule as a switch.
* **Instances up to the replacements** (r27.35, asked for). A step applies one rule, so a conditional that needs a rule *and then* a replacement states neither: "¬(P ∨ ¬P) → (P ∧ ¬P)" is De Morgan's and a commutation, and by default it needs support. Switch on **instances up to the replacements** (under *Extra rule instances*) and the replacements that are on may be taken on the way: that conditional is then De Morgan's, so is "¬(P ∧ Q) → (¬Q ∨ ¬P)", and "((P ∧ Q) ∧ R) → (R ∧ (Q ∧ P))" is commutation. The explanation says the order or grouping was changed. Only what the replacements allow: with commutation switched off the order is content again, and nothing invalid follows -- "(P ∨ Q) → (Q ∧ P)" and "¬(P ∨ Q) → (¬P ∨ ¬Q)" stay out, as does a restatement.
* **"Poe is Edgar" is an identity** (September 23). "Is" and a name, rather than "is" and a property: so identity symmetry, transitivity and substitution reach it, as they always did "a = b". The reader still builds it as a predication, so "every raven other than Fido" is still matched by "Bob is not Fido".

  Capitalized and still no name: what somebody is of a nation, a faith, a party, a school or a period. "Bob is Greek", "Bob is Bavarian", "Bob is Republican", "Bob is Martian" and "Bob is Portuguese" say what Bob is like, so none of them gives "Republican is tall" from "Bob is tall"; the endings *-ist* and *-ese* belong to no given name and are taken as well. "Poe is a raven" keeps its article and stays a predication. No list of words can be complete, so the reading says what it did: *"Edgar" is read as a name: this says Poe and Edgar are one thing, not what Poe is like*.

  **A name goes into what is said of a thing, whatever the words are.** "Poe is Edgar" and "Poe sings" give "Edgar sings", as do "Poe wrote a poem", "Poe did not sing" and "Poe wants a raven" -- before, only a property the check had parsed into symbols went through. What is said of the *name* does not: "Poe has four letters", "Poe rhymes with doe", "Poe was believed to be dead" and "Poe has been called Eddy" keep the name they were given, since Giorgione is so called because of his size and Barbarelli is not. The one who believes is not read through what is believed: "Bob is Robert" and "Bob believes that Mary is happy" give "Robert believes that Mary is happy", while "Bob believes that Poe is a poet" does not give it of Edgar.

  The name goes into the other place of an open relation as well: "Mary is taller than Poe", "Mary admires Poe" and "Mary is a friend of Poe" are about Edgar as much. Not where what is wanted may be wanted under a name -- "Mary worships Zeus", "is afraid of Zeus", "looks for Zeus", "wants Zeus" -- nor into a clause of its own, which says how somebody put it ("Mary said that Poe sings", "heard that Poe sings"). The check's own lists of intensional verbs and attitude adjectives draw that line. What is necessary or possible of a thing, and what is so in a story, keep their name too ("Ada is necessarily tall", "Ada is a fictional character"). Derive Parent offers the substitution in the map's own words.
* **A rule instance follows the rules that are on** (September 23). A conditional that states a step needs no support while that step's rule is switched on: "¬(P ∨ ¬P) → (¬P ∧ ¬¬P)" states De Morgan's, "((P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬P) → Q" disjunctive syllogism's. Before, only the core steps counted. Switch the rule off and the conditional needs support again. Two license nothing: tautological consequence, which would make every tautology need no support, and a claim restated, which applies no rule.
* **Two dilemmas, named apart** (September 23). "A or B", "if A then C", "if B then C" give C: that is **proof by cases**, which is what a map has in place of ∨ elimination, and it is a core step. "If A then B", "if C then D", "A or C" give "B or D": that is the **constructive dilemma** proper, a familiar step. The names now match the textbooks, as do **double-negation elimination** ("¬¬A gives A") and **double-negation replacement** (a pair added or removed at any depth) -- "negation elimination" belongs to deriving ⊥ from A and ¬A.
* **One reductio.** The basis takes the form with two conditionals: a claim that implies both something and its denial is false. Its other forms are familiar steps, each switchable on its own: **negation introduction** ("if A, then ⊥" gives "not A"), **indirect proof** ("if not A, then ⊥" gives A) and **consequentia mirabilis** ("if A, then not A" gives "not A"). They are derivable from the basis one -- "(A → ⊥) → ¬A" takes nine lines -- but not the other way round: the ⊥ form never uses its condition twice, and a map has no subproof in which to. In Łukasiewicz's three values every other basis rule holds while the two-conditional form fails, which is the same reason absorption fails there.
* **One rule per step, with nothing done silently.**
  * **Rule instances.** A rule instance is a conditional whose antecedent is the rule's premises, conjoined, and whose consequent is the rule's conclusion. "(A ∧ ¬A) → ⊥" is Contradiction's instance. "A → (¬A → ⊥)" is none: that form is exportation's work. Only the basis gives rule instances.
  * **Closures.** "∀x (B(x) → ∃y B(y))" is the closure of the instance "B(a) → ∃y B(y)", for a name found nowhere else. It is marked as a closure, not as an instance of the rule.
  * **Conditionals.** A conditional is read by its written main condition. "If A, and if B, then C" is "if A, then if B, then C". Modus ponens discharges one condition, the first; discharging the second first takes commutation too. Modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, reductio and the dilemmas read each conditional the same way.
  * **Several conditions at once.** "If A, and if B, then C" says what "if A and B, then C" says (exportation), and the familiar step **modus ponens, conditions together** (on by default) takes them that way: give the conditions one by one, or give the conjuncts of a single "and" condition, and the step is one. Switched off, each condition takes its own modus ponens, and a conjunction its own conjunction introduction. Rule instances stay with the basis either way: "((A → B) ∧ A) → B" is one, "((A → (B → C)) ∧ A ∧ B) → C" is not.
  * **Biconditionals.** A biconditional is its own claim. "P ↔ Q" is not "(P → Q) ∧ (Q → P)"; material equivalence takes the one to the other, and conjunction elimination does not apply to a biconditional.
  * **Quantified denials in symbols.** In symbols, a quantified claim is denied as written. "¬∀x F(x)" is not "∃x ¬F(x)"; quantifier negation takes the one to the other, in one named step, as it does for "not all ravens are black" and "some raven is not black".
  * **Quantifier distribution and the vacuous quantifier** apply, in the basis, to a whole formula, and in their ∀ forms only: "∀x (A → B)" gives "∀x A → ∀x B" (Enderton's third axiom), and P gives "∀x P" (his fourth). Their ∃ forms -- "∃x A → ∃x B", and "∃x P" gives P -- follow from existential elimination, and, with their use inside a formula, are the extensions "quantifier distribution, anywhere" and "vacuous quantifier, anywhere" (both on by default).
* **Extensions.** Every other rule is derivable from the basis. The extensions come in groups that a map switches on and off in the Logic panel:
  * **Intro and elimination steps** (on): conjunction introduction and elimination, disjunction introduction, the constructive dilemma, both biconditional rules, reductio, ¬¬ elimination, Contradiction, explosion, universal elimination, existential introduction and elimination, the identity rules, hypothetical syllogism, exportation, quantifier distribution, the vacuous quantifier -- and rule instances. These were the basis until September 22; each is derived from the axioms now.
  * **Familiar steps** (on): modus ponens with its conditions together, modus tollens, disjunctive and conjunctive syllogism, the constructive and destructive dilemmas, resolution, absorption, negation introduction, indirect proof, consequentia mirabilis, universal modus ponens and tollens, the universal, existential and categorical syllogisms, subalternation, identity symmetry and transitivity, and predicate congruence.
  * **Replacement rules** (on): De Morgan, transposition, material implication and equivalence, negated conditional and biconditional, conversion, contraposition, and double-negation replacement (a pair of negations added or removed, at any depth). A rule's own result could always lose a pair, so "¬(P ∨ ¬P)" gives "¬P ∧ P" by De Morgan whatever that switch says; what the switch adds is putting a pair in, and taking one out on its own.
  * **Order and grouping** (on): commutation, association, idempotence, distribution, Boolean absorption ("A and (A or B)" is A) and the ⊥ laws ("A or ⊥" is A; "A and ⊥" is ⊥; "A and not A" is ⊥).
  * **Quantifier equivalences** (on): change of bound variable, quantifier negation, quantifier commutation, the rules of passage, and quantifier distribution and the vacuous quantifier, anywhere.
  * **Rules inside quantifiers** (on): a rule applied to what a quantifier says ("conjunction elimination under ∃").
  * **Negations anywhere** (off): double-negation elimination anywhere in a formula, in any positive even number.
  * **Extra rule instances** (off): "P → P", "P ∨ ¬P" and "not (P and not P)".
  * **Catch-all steps** (off): **tautological consequence** -- the conclusion true on every row of the truth table where the premises are, with every part that is not ∧ ∨ ¬ → ↔ ⊥ counted as a letter -- and **equivalence replacement**, one part swapped for a part that says the same by truth tables, at any depth. They are tried after every named rule, so a step that has a name keeps it. Together they cover every truth-functional step and every propositional equivalence, named or not.
* **Change of bound variable.** "∃x B(x)" and "∃y B(y)" are, in the end, one claim, and a map can show it. With change of bound variable on (the default), they are the same claim: a step from one to the other is certified by that rule, and "∃x B(x) → ∃y B(y)" is its rule instance. With it off, and whenever the basis alone is checked, bound variables are part of a formula, and the one is derived from the other.
* **The Logic panel** sits at the bottom of the Evaluation Overview and takes at most a third of it. It shows the base logic (Standard; intuitionistic and modal logics are listed for later), the basis, and the groups. Each group has a switch, and opening it shows a switch for each rule. The settings are saved with the map and shared with its collaborators; the newest change wins. They are settings, not edits: undo leaves them alone, so undoing one's own edit never reverts a collaborator's switch. Changing one re-checks the map.
* **Order and grouping are part of a claim.** "Alice sings and Bob dances" and "Bob dances and Alice sings" are different claims. So are "if P, and if Q, then R" and "if Q, and if P, then R", and "A and (B and C)" and "(A and B) and C". Commutation, association and idempotence make the one the other in one step. With them off, the long way still works: each part by conjunction elimination, then conjunction introduction in the new order.
* **Derivations from the basis alone**, with every extension off:
  * ¬¬A from A. "(A ∧ ¬A) → ⊥" is a rule instance (Contradiction). Exportation gives "A → (¬A → ⊥)", modus ponens "¬A → ⊥", and reductio "¬¬A".
  * ∃y B(y) from ∃x B(x), in three lines. "∀x (B(x) → ∃y B(y))" is the closure of an existential introduction instance; existential elimination detaches "∃y B(y)".
  * ∀y P(y) from ∀x P(x), in five lines. "∀y ((∀x P(x)) → P(y))" is the closure of a universal elimination instance; quantifier distribution gives "(∀y ∀x P(x)) → ∀y P(y)"; the vacuous quantifier takes the premise to "∀y ∀x P(x)"; modus ponens ends it. This is what the vacuous quantifier is for: it puts a premise that says nothing about y under a "∀y", where distribution can reach it.
  * ∀x (F → H) from ∀x (F → G) and ∀x (G → H), in ten lines. The steps are closures of a hypothetical syllogism instance and an exportation instance, then quantifier distribution and modus ponens.
  * P → P, in fourteen lines: "(P ∧ ¬P) → ⊥" (Contradiction), exportation, the derived "(¬P → ⊥) → ¬¬P", hypothetical syllogism, "¬¬P → P" (Negation Elimination), hypothetical syllogism.
  * P → (P ∧ P), in thirty-three lines, which is absorption's own work. "(P ∧ P) → (P ∧ P)" is proved as above; exportation gives "P → (P → (P ∧ P))"; and the reductio that joins two conditions -- supposing "P ∧ ¬(P ∧ P)" as a condition -- gives "P → (P ∧ P)".
  * P ∨ ¬P, in thirty-nine lines. With N for ¬(P ∨ ¬P), "N → ¬P", so "N → (P ∨ ¬P)" and "N → ¬N"; reductio gives ¬N, and Negation Elimination gives P ∨ ¬P (`logical-theorem-r27-test.js`).
* **Rules inside quantifiers, in English.** English claims have first-order forms, so a rule applies to what a quantifier says. For example:
  * "Some raven is black" gives "Something is black";
  * "Every raven is black" and "Every raven is old" give "Every raven is black and old";
  * "Every raven is a black bird" gives "Every raven is black";
  * "Some philosopher is wise" and "Every philosopher is a person" give "Some person is wise".

  "Everything is black" gives "Every raven is black" in two steps: disjunction introduction under ∀ ("Everything is a non-raven or black"), then material implication under ∀.
* **People, and things: no existential import.** "Everyone loves Fido" is "∀x (person(x) → x loves Fido)", true if there are no people, so it does not give "Someone loves Fido". The same holds of every kind: "Every raven is black" does not give "Some raven is black". With a person named it goes through: "Everyone loves Fido" and "Fido is a person" give "Fido loves Fido" (universal modus ponens), and "Fido is a person and Fido loves Fido" gives "Someone loves Fido". "Everything is black" gives "Something is black" only the standard way, through a name: "(∀x F(x)) → F(a)" (universal elimination) and "F(a) → ∃x F(x)" (existential introduction), hypothetical syllogism, modus ponens. The other way round, "everything" does give "everyone": "Everything is black" gives "Everyone is black" in the two steps that take it to "Every raven is black" -- disjunction introduction under ∀, then material implication. Where the predicate is a verb phrase ("Everything loves Fido"), the middle line ("Everything is a non-person or loves Fido") is not read as one predicate, so that route stops in English while it still works in symbols.
* **Exceptions.** "Every raven except Fido is black" (or "but Fido", "except for Fido", "everyone except Fido") is read "every raven other than Fido is black". So "Every raven except Fido is black", "Bob is a raven" and "Bob is not Fido" give "Bob is black". Von Fintel (1993) holds that it also says Fido is a raven and is not black; that reading is flagged and named.
* **Denials in the object.** "A raven that Poe does not love" and "every raven that Poe loves" are read with the object left open. "Every raven that Poe loves is black", "Fido is a raven" and "Poe loves Fido" give "Fido is black". Quantifier negation relates two pairs:
  * "Poe does not love every raven" and "There is a raven that Poe does not love";
  * "Poe loves no raven" and "There is no raven that Poe loves".
* **Verbs joined in a relative clause.** "Every bird that sings or dances is happy" is about each bird that sings or dances. With "Fido is a bird" and "Fido sings or dances" it gives "Fido is happy". Each side must be a single verb for the "or" to be split. In "every bird that loves ravens or crows" the "or" may join the objects, or join "loves ravens" with "crows" read as a verb, so that phrase is left whole.
* **"Person" is a kind** (soundness fix). "Every person that sings is happy" and "Fido sings" gave "Fido is happy", because "person" was read as "thing". The step now needs "Fido is a person", and "all who sing" is about persons.
* **Scope is flagged, not settled.** On September 21, "it is not the case that A and B" was read as denying all of it, which guessed the wide scope. Each of these is now flagged:
  * "It is not the case that A and B" (or "A or B") may deny all of it, or only A.
  * "It is not the case that Q if P" may deny the conditional, or only Q.
  * "Poe does not love each raven" may mean not every raven, or none.
  * "Poe does not love a raven" may mean that he loves none, or that there is one he does not love. The same holds after a modal ("Poe cannot beat a player"). "Poe does not love a single raven" says the first. A predicate noun is no object ("Poe is not a raven").
  * "Bob does not think that P" may mean that Bob has no such thought, or that he thinks it is not so. This covers think, believe, suppose, expect, imagine, reckon and feel.
  * "Poe does not love Fido and Bob" may mean not both, or neither.

  The flag names both readings and says which one would make the step follow. "Both", "either" and "neither" settle the scope: "it is not the case that both A and B". A flagged clause is left whole, so a step that holds of it whatever it means, such as modus tollens on it as it stands, is still certified.
* **What a denial in a word takes in** (soundness fix). A denial in a phrase's first word ("non-raven", "impossible") reached past an "and" or "or", and into a relative clause. "Everything is a non-raven or black" was read "nothing is a raven or black", and "a non-raven that is black" was read "not a raven that is black". Two invalid steps were certified as a result:
  * "Everything is a non-raven or black ⊢ Nothing is a raven or black";
  * "Every non-raven that is black is a crow; Fido is not a raven that is black ⊢ Fido is a crow".

  The denial now reaches as far as the word's own complement and no further: "impossible to know", "unaware of the danger", "a non-member of the club".
* **A junction after a relative clause** (soundness fix). "Everything that is a raven or black is a crow" was split at the "or" into "everything that is a raven" and "black is a crow". "Fido is a bird that is black or white" was read as "Fido is a black bird, or Fido is white". So "Black is a crow ⊢ Everything that is a raven or black is a crow" and "Fido is white ⊢ Fido is a bird that is black or white" were certified. Three rules now apply:
  * A quantifier phrase says something only with a verb after its relative clause.
  * A junction after an open relative clause joins claims only when what follows has a subject of its own.
  * "Or" after an open relative clause in a predicate is flagged, with both readings named.
* **"Or" and "and" in a predicate, said of one thing.** "Every raven is black or white" and "Fido is a raven" now give "Fido is black or white" by universal modus ponens. Universal elimination and universal modus tollens work the same way: "Fido is neither black nor white" gives "Fido is not a raven".

### September 21: qualifiers, positions, quantified objects, adjectives, plurals, first-order coverage

* **Qualifiers are never dropped or folded into a subject.** Before this pass, "I think that Poe is a raven" and "All ravens are black" gave "I think that Poe is black" by universal modus ponens. "I think that Poe is black" gave "Something is black". The same was true of "probably", "it seems that", "supposedly", "allegedly" and "according to Bob". The reader now distinguishes:
  * **Probability hedges.** "Probably", "presumably", "likely", "it is likely that", "chances are", "there is a good chance that", "it might be that" and "in all likelihood" say that P's denial has not been established, as "perhaps" does. "Unlikely" says that P has not been. They serve as weak challenges.
  * **Possibility.** "Possibly" and "it is possible that" may be epistemic or about what could be, and are flagged.
  * **Speaker and frame words.** "Apparently", "allegedly", "clearly", "certainly", "usually" and "necessarily" in front, and phrases like "according to Bob", "in Hamlet", "in functionalism" and "on Kant's view", make the sentence one claim.
  * **Attitude verbs.** "Bob thinks (that) P", "it seems that P" and "Bob told Ann that P" say something of Bob or of "it". The thought is part of the predicate: "everyone thinks that P" and "Bob is a person" give "Bob thinks that P". An "and" inside what is thought is never split off; with a comma it is flagged.
  * **Possible conditions and contrasts.** "When", "given that", "in case", "while" and "only when" are flagged, not read as "if" or "and".
  * **Quotations.** Quotations that no reading takes up make the sentence one claim.
  * **One argument.** "The argument with premises “A” and “B” is unsound" is read as a claim about that argument; before, it was split at the "and".
* **Position.** A qualifier, frame or attitude stands for the same claim wherever it sits: "P, probably" is "probably P"; "P, according to Bob" is "according to Bob, P"; "P, Bob thinks" and "Poe, Bob thinks, is black" are "Bob thinks that P". Different words are still different claims ("likely" is not "probable"). `language-r27-qualifier-test.js` checks 165 phrasings in their classes. The scratch harness checked 410.
* **Quantifier words are logic.** "All ravens are black", "every raven is black", "each raven is black" and "whatever is a raven is black" are one claim, so modus ponens accepts one where the other is written. Terms keep their spelling and capitals. Different quantifiers ("some" against "all") stay different, and a generic ("Ravens are black") is still flagged. A step whose wordings differ by a denial ("not all ravens are black" / "some raven is not black") is still named quantifier negation.
* **Quantified objects.** Quantifier words inside a predicate are logic too. "Loves every raven", "loves all (the) ravens", "loves each raven", "loves each and every raven", "loves every single raven" and free-choice "loves any raven" are one predicate. "Loves some raven", "loves a raven" and "loves at least one raven" are another. "Everyone", "everybody", "all people" and "every person" are one, and so are "someone", "somebody", "some person" and "a person"; likewise the "-thing" words. These stay distinct:
  * "every" and "some";
  * "the ravens" and bare "ravens" (not "every raven");
  * "not every" and "no";
  * a verb that can take its object together ("gathered all the sheep", "outnumbers all the ravens"), which keeps "all": there it can mean all at once.

  Denials hold: "loves no raven" denies "loves some raven", and "does not love any raven" denies "loves a raven". A floating-quantifier misread was fixed: "Poe loves all ravens" had been read as "all Poes love ravens".
* **Objects as relations.** A quantified object gives its case, by the rules that give a subject's, with the case in the object's place:
  * universal modus ponens: "Poe loves every raven" and "Fido is a raven" give "Poe loves Fido"; "Poe loves no raven" and "Fido is a raven" give "Poe does not love Fido";
  * universal modus tollens: "Poe loves every raven" and "Poe does not love Fido" give "Fido is not a raven";
  * existential introduction: "Poe loves Fido and Fido is a raven" gives "Poe loves a raven" (the case goes in as one conjunction, as for a subject);
  * universal elimination: "Poe loves everything" gives "Poe loves Fido";
  * under a subject's quantifier: "Every philosopher loves every raven" and "Fido is a raven" give "Every philosopher loves Fido" (*universal modus ponens under ∀*), and likewise "no philosopher loves any raven" and "some philosopher loves every raven".

  This holds of a verb's object ("gives every raven a worm"), and of the object of "is" and a preposition ("is taller than", "is a friend of", "is loved by"). "Is afraid of all ravens" is now read as "is afraid of every raven". Derive Parent offers the case in the map's words ("Poe loves Fido").

  A relative clause is part of the kind. "Poe loves every raven that sings" and "Fido is a raven that sings" (or "Fido is a raven and Fido sings") give "Poe loves Fido". "Fido is a raven" alone does not, and "all ravens that sing are black" works the same way for a subject. After "to" and a verb that says the act was done ("manage", "happen", "bother", "dare", "remember", "get"), the object passes through: "Poe manages to love every raven" gives "Poe manages to love Fido".

  Where the case holds on one reading only, the step is flagged as ambiguous. The flag names the reading ("said of each raven there is") and says the step follows by universal modus ponens on it:
  * after a modal ("must love every raven": of each raven there is, it must be loved, or it must be that all ravens are?);
  * in the past or perfect ("fed every raven", "has read every book": the ravens then, or the ravens now?);
  * with a verb of wanting, seeking, fearing or depicting, or an adjective of attitude ("seeks every unicorn", "wants to meet every raven", "is afraid of every raven": the object may be taken under a description);
  * after a noun and "of" ("the mother of every raven": one mother of them all, or each raven's own?).

  Verbs of saying and believing with a that-clause ("Poe says every raven is black") are not read into, and the case is not given. The case is also not given after a denial ("does not love every raven"), with "almost" ("almost every raven") or with an exception ("every raven but Fido"), as these give no case on any reading.

  "Every philosopher loves a raven" is still flagged for its quantifier order (one raven for all, or one each?), as before.
* **Adjectives.** Following Kamp and Partee (1995) and Partee (2010), an adjective splits only when it says something of the thing itself (intersective). "Poe is a black raven" is "Poe is black and Poe is a raven". So is "Poe is a raven that is black": a relative clause always splits. The phrase gives each part by conjunction elimination and follows from them by conjunction introduction. A universal's subject or predicate is matched by its parts, so "all black ravens are birds" is "all ravens that are black are birds", and "every raven is a black bird" gives "Poe is a black bird" of a raven. The other kinds:
  * **Subsective.** "A good pianist" is a pianist, but may be good only as a pianist. This covers gradable words ("a small elephant"), relational ones ("a moral philosopher", "a criminal lawyer", "a Greek teacher"), traits of a doer ("a careful driver"), "true" of anything but a claim ("a true friend") and non-predicative words ("a total stranger", "the main reason"). The noun holds; the adjective alone is flagged.
  * **Privative and non-subsective.** For "a fake gun", "an alleged thief", "a former president", "a possible murderer" and "a false dilemma", neither the noun nor the adjective holds, and both are flagged.
  * **Materials.** "A stone lion" may be a likeness of a lion. It is treated as privative.
  * **Idioms.** "A black hole", "a white lie", "a red herring" and "a hot dog" are read whole, and flagged.
  * **Unknown words.** A word the lists do not know is read whole and flagged. Such words are often nouns: "a zombie argument", "a police dog".

  A flag names the reading that would make the step follow ("Read as “a pianist that is good”, the step follows by conjunction elimination"). The lists hold about 970 adjectives and 100 idioms. There are more non-intersective adjectives than any list holds, so an unknown word is flagged rather than split.
* **Adjective misreads fixed.**
  * "Poe is a non-black raven" was read as "Poe is not a black raven". "The argument is not a valid argument" was read as "The argument is an invalid argument". "All non-black ravens are albinos" was read as being about everything that is not a black raven. A denial word before a noun now denies the adjective only.
  * "Black ravens fly" was read as a thing called "black" that "ravens fly". "Some black ravens fly" and "Ravens are black and noisy birds" were misread in the same way. A known adjective now marks the noun phrase. "Evil exists" and "evil causes suffering" still have "evil" as the subject.
  * "A good and faithful servant" was read with "good" detached from "servant". Adjectives joined before one noun now say the noun once ("a black and white raven": black, white, and a raven).
  * A definite description ("Poe is the black raven") is not split, since uniqueness is more than "black and a raven". The past ("Poe was a black raven") is not split either, since "was black" and "was a raven" may be true at different times.
* **No "tautology" replacement rule.** Each direction of it is another rule:
  * P ∧ P gives P by conjunction elimination.
  * P, taken twice, gives P ∧ P by conjunction introduction.
  * P gives P ∨ P by disjunction introduction.
  * P ∨ P gives P by constructive dilemma on "P → P", taken twice.

  Since September 22, idempotence is a rule too, in the Order and grouping extension (on by default). The routes above are the basis's. "P → P" is not a rule instance. It is proved from the basis in six lines: "(P ∧ ¬P) → ⊥", exportation, "(¬P → ⊥) → ¬¬P", hypothetical syllogism, "¬¬P → P", hypothetical syllogism. "P ∨ ¬P" follows from it by material implication, a replacement rule; from the basis alone it takes 17 lines. The derivations still go through, line by line.
* **Absorption with a conjunction.** "If A, then B and C" now gives "if A, then A, B and C". The conjunction is read flat, as English lists are. The symbolic fuzzer had found this gap (seed 7). Split adjectives make it common: "if Poe is a raven, then Poe is a black bird".
* **Tests.** `language-r27-adjective-test.js` checks the splits and the flags, what stays apart, and the readings. It also runs a soundness fuzz: 2,000 steps shaped like the rules, judged by the same three meanings (split, noun and whole, whole). None of the 447 certified is invalid. `language-r27-qualifier-test.js` covers quantified objects.
* **Speed.** Normalized text and terms are cached, and the quoted-warrant patterns are built once and skipped when a sentence has no quotation. The wording-near-miss pass compares only claims that share enough words. The benchmark of 580 uncached steps went from 2.5 s to 1.0 s, and the slowest step from 119 ms to 49 ms.
* **Plurals.** Nouns ending in a vowel and -s (trees, horses, cases, ideas, photos, premises) did not singularize before this pass, so "All trees are plants" and "Oscar is a tree" did not certify. The -ze, -che and -o endings (sizes, caches, shoes) were also mishandled. The rules are now one set for reading and for writing conclusions, with short lists of exceptions:
  * singulars ending as plurals do (gas, atlas, herpes, cosmos);
  * plurals ending as singulars do (taxis, menus);
  * -che and -sse nouns (caches, posses);
  * -o nouns taking -es (mosquitoes);
  * the -sis family (prostheses).

  Verbs follow the same rules ("freezes" freeze, "aches" ache, "tiptoes" tiptoe, "goes" go). "Do" with an object is the verb, not the auxiliary: "Poe did not do the dishes" denies "Poe did the dishes".
* **Textbook forms.** These now read:
  * "only if P is Q" (inverted);
  * "P, otherwise Q";
  * "ravens are all black" (but not "the ravens are all black");
  * "the only Fs are Gs" and "Gs are the only Fs";
  * gerunds as propositions ("Poe's being a raven implies that Poe is black", "... is sufficient for Poe being black", "... is equivalent to ...");
  * "suppose P; then Q".
* **Wording.** A step that would follow if two claims were worded alike now says which words differ. The screenshot's case is covered: "Worded the same way each time, this would follow by modus ponens, but ‘... flashing lights can not produce consciousness’ and ‘... flashing lights and control circuitry can not produce consciousness’ differ: “and control circuitry” is in one and not the other."
* **Rules added.** Categorical syllogism (the fifteen valid moods, named by mood), conjunctive syllogism, biconditional introduction, identity introduction, vacuous quantifier, rules of passage, and quantifier distribution. Beyond those:
  * any rule under a shared quantifier;
  * quantified rule instances;
  * replacement inside quantifiers;
  * substitution into any first-order formula;
  * all four forms of quantifier negation.

  Conversion and contraposition now come before the propositional replacements, so they keep their names. See `COMPLETENESS-r27.md` for the completeness argument and the tests.

### September 20 follow-up

The new `language-r27-audit-test.js` checks actual inference recognition as well as parsing. It exercises front/trailing conditionals, necessary/sufficient condition direction, negation wrappers, contraction and quotation variants, epistemic phrase position, source-backed past alternatives, every imported noun's universal instance, and rejection of existential import.

* `P is sufficient for Q`, `P is a sufficient condition for Q`, and `A sufficient condition for Q is P` now work with proposition letters as well as quoted clauses. Necessary conditions reverse the direction; necessary-and-sufficient conditions retain both directions. Ordinary noun phrases such as `oxygen is necessary for life` are not treated as named propositions.
* `In the case that P, Q` and `Q in the case that P` share the conditional reading. `In case`, causal claims and reports of someone's supposition are not automatically equated with material implication.
* Delimited `P is untrue` and `P is not untrue` preserve their polarity. Matching now compares the operands of negation rather than requiring identical wording for the negation wrapper.
* Passive hedges such as `It cannot be established that P` and `That P cannot be established` match. This includes the registered passive verbs, contractions, `has yet to be ...`, `remains to be ...`, and unestablished/unproved/undetermined status forms. `We are unable/not able to establish that P` matches `We cannot establish that P`. Different speakers, `not yet shown` versus `cannot be shown`, and inability to rule out versus inability to conclude remain distinct.
* Conditional boundaries now respect parentheses and quotations, including British single quotation marks. A nested antecedent such as `If (it cannot be established that if P, then Q), then R` can be used in modus ponens. An ungrouped embedded conditional with ambiguous `then` attachment is flagged instead of silently split at the first `then`. This is a specific boundary repair, not a complete solution to English scope.
* Eight additional past-tense families are verified against the pinned verb snapshot: mislearn, misspell, outleap, overspill, plead, relearn, relight and rewake. Reviewed past alternatives also match in actual conditional inferences; present/perfect distinctions and participant names remain significant. All 281 automatically imported noun mappings are tested in universal modus ponens and for the absence of existential import.
* Four explicit dictionary-backed noun mappings supplement the snapshot: octopi→octopus, dwarves→dwarf, zeros→zero and zeroes→zero. Their individual source URLs are retained in `language-data/reviewed-variants.json`. The audit also checks 28 already-supported alternate or invariant plural forms. This does not settle which sense of a noun a user intends.
* Explosion now accepts a single `⊥` premise, in addition to a claim and its negation. The existing warrant assessment still rejects contradiction-based support. Biconditional introduction is already covered by conjunction introduction because the parser represents it as the two converse conditionals. Reductio is used by the explicit excluded-middle proof and is retained. No registered rule was removed, and no duplicate biconditional rule or unrestricted assumption-discharge rule was added.

References rechecked: [Ginger's irregular verbs](https://www.gingersoftware.com/content/grammar-rules/verbs/list-of-irregular-verbs), [the irregular-plurals source](https://github.com/sindresorhus/irregular-plurals/blob/main/irregular-plurals.json), [Cambridge's conditional expressions](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/uk/grammar/british-grammar/conditionals-other), and [forall x's basic classical rules](https://forallx.openlogicproject.org/html/Ch17.html). The licensed, pinned datasets remain the reproducible import sources; see `language-data/sources.json` for commits and hashes. No finite dictionary or suite covers all English expressions or disambiguates all word senses.

Negation-distribution policy: a named replacement may be followed by removal of any number of negation pairs anywhere in its resulting formula. Partial simplification on independent branches is allowed; introducing extra pairs is not. This applies to De Morgan, Negated Conditional, Negated Biconditional, transposition, material implication/equivalence, contraposition, and quantifier negation. The quantifier replacement preserves bindings and swaps universal/existential quantifiers; it does not also distribute the resulting negation through another connective in the same step. Opaque warrant reports are not rewritten internally.

For Negated Conditional, the expanded result of `~(P -> ~Q)` is `~~P & ~~Q`. All four choices that leave either or both pairs in place are accepted with the same label; adding four negations to P or Q is not. Negated Biconditional uses the exclusive-disjunction form `(P & ~Q) or (~P & Q)`, with the same optional pair-removal policy. Partial De Morgan simplifications work on longer conjunctions/disjunctions as well, without enumerating every combination in advance.

Negation Elimination is double negation elimination (September 21): `~~A` gives `A` when the two negations are the main connective, as a step and as a rule instance. It only removes. `~~~~P` gives `~~P`, not `P`; a pair inside a part (`~~P or Q`) is not removed; and `A` does not give `~~A` by it. The double negation replacement rule (one pair added or removed anywhere) and the earlier Negation Elimination (any even number removed anywhere) are now the Negations anywhere extension, off by default. They give no rule instances. `~~A` is derived from `A` by the basis alone:
  1. `(A & ~A) -> ⊥` is a rule instance (Contradiction).
  2. Exportation gives `A -> (~A -> ⊥)`. (That conditional is no rule instance itself: from `A`, `~A -> ⊥` follows by no one rule.)
  3. With `A`, modus ponens gives `~A -> ⊥`.
  4. Reductio gives `~~A`.

Constructive Dilemma already covers shared-consequent proof-by-cases arguments; there is no separate Proof by Cases rule to remove.

Material Implication retains the literal new negation when converting a disjunction to a conditional, and also permits removing even pairs: both `~~P v (~P v Q)` and `~~~~P v (~P v Q)` give `~~~P -> (~P v Q)`.

Recognized classical rule instances written as conditionals can be verified without assuming their antecedents. A conjunction in the antecedent can provide a rule's joint premises. A restatement such as `P -> P` is not a rule instance, because "same claim" is not a rule of the package, and it is proved in the map. The rule instance `P -> (P v P)` (disjunction introduction) gives `P -> (P & (P v P))` by absorption. The rule instance `(P & (P v P)) -> P` (conjunction elimination) then gives `P -> P` by hypothetical syllogism. Only the basis gives rule instances (September 22), so `~(P -> P) -> (P & ~P)`, a negated conditional, is not one. Nor is `P -> ~~P`, since double negation is an extension; it is proved from `(P & ~P) -> ⊥` (Contradiction) by exportation, then `(~P -> ⊥) -> ~~P` (reductio) and hypothetical syllogism. A standalone tautology that isn't a conditional, such as Excluded Middle (`P v ~P`), needs support like any claim. It follows from `P -> P` in one step by material implication, a replacement rule (on by default), and from the basis alone in 17 lines. This is a conservative rule-instance check, not a complete theorem prover; modal/warrant claims and ambiguous readings are not automatically certified. Verified formulas receive a green Rule Instance tag centered across the bottom border while Deductive is active, including in image exports. Recognition is recalculated from the text, not stored as a user-controlled axiom flag. Verified rule instances supply warrant, subject to the map's existing challenge assessment.

`⊥` (also `\bot`) is the contradiction constant. Contradiction derives it from `P` and `~P`, or from `P & ~P`. Reductio accepts `~C -> ⊥` as a route to `C`. `(P & ~P) -> ⊥` is a verified rule instance. Since September 22 `~(P v ~P) -> (P & ~P)` is not, because only the basis gives rule instances. `~(P v ~P) -> ⊥` is derived from the basis, and it supports `P v ~P` by reductio.

Rich Text is off by default for new edits. Existing saved formatting is preserved. Runs of logical tildes are protected from strikethrough interpretation in both rendering and logical text cleanup; ordinary paired `~~strikethrough~~` still works when formatting is enabled.

Conditional markers now share a vocabulary across leading and trailing positions: if, provided (that), providing (that), as long as, so long as, on (the) condition that, in the event that, on the assumption that, assuming (that), supposing (that), and whenever. Leading forms accept an optional then; trailing forms accept a separating comma. In particular, `Q, so long as P` is not mistaken for `P, so Q`. Ambiguous attachment still produces an ambiguity flag. Moving words arbitrarily is not meaning-preserving: only-if direction, quantifier scope, negation, and anaphora remain significant.

`Q unless P` and `Unless P, Q` mean inclusive `Q or P`. Logical tilde negation now survives English text cleanup; only paired Markdown strikethrough is removed. Symbolic subclauses are recognized inside English connectives, including `~P`, `¬P`, and `\neg P`. Thus `Q unless ~P` with `P` gives Q, whereas the same unless claim with `~P` does not. The latter has counterexample P=false, Q=false. A symbolic operator or explicit grouping is required when parsing a symbolic subclause, so ordinary names such as Poe are not mistaken for compact predicate notation P(o,e).

Added single-step classical resolution: `P or Q`, `not P or R` entail `Q or R`. Only one complementary pair is removed; remaining disjuncts must be preserved. Derive Parent can suggest this conclusion. Added explosion from a claim and its outright negation; an epistemic hedge does not qualify as a negation. Explosion certifies validity only: contradictory premises cannot jointly supply warrant, and Derive Parent does not invent arbitrary conclusions from them.

Reductio is retained and tested: `P → Q` and `P → ¬Q` give `¬P`; `P → ¬P` gives `¬P`. No rules were removed. Existing rules already cover modus ponens/tollens, hypothetical syllogism, both dilemmas (including shared-conclusion cases), conjunction/disjunction rules, biconditional elimination, double negation, equivalence transformations, identity substitution, quantifier instances, and categorical syllogisms. These are a finite set of single-step patterns, not a complete natural-deduction proof editor. General assumption discharge, arbitrary-object universal introduction, fresh-witness existential elimination, and proofs beyond the recognized premise-free rule instances still require explicit proof-context support; tree ancestry alone is not a discharged supposition. (Since September 21–22, rule instances with hypothetical syllogism, absorption, exportation, quantifier distribution and the vacuous quantifier do this work without suppositions; see `COMPLETENESS-r27.md`.)

The morphology audit checks every entry in the pinned upstream lists (363 verb bases and 419 noun entries), imported mappings, reviewed spelling alternatives, participles, and exclusions. The upstream noun list was also inspected online; this pass does not replace the pinned snapshots or claim that all English senses are covered. The lists remain those from [RosaeNLG](https://github.com/RosaeNLG/rosaenlg/tree/master/packages/english-verbs-irregular) and [irregular-plurals](https://github.com/sindresorhus/irregular-plurals). Rule review uses the [Open Logic Project's classical natural-deduction presentation](https://forallx.openlogicproject.org/html/Ch17.html).

`language-r27-position-rules-test.js` adds positional equivalence and fallacy checks, hedge contractions with conclude/infer/establish/determine, resolution and explosion examples, Reductio retention, a map-level explosion warrant check, and all eight truth assignments for 216 small resolution instances. ## Additional constructions

Truth wrappers are transparent in Standard classical mode: `it is true that P`, `it is the case that P`, and `it is not false that P` express P; `it is false that P`, `it is not true that P`, and `it is not the case that P` express its denial. Contractions are supported. Suffix forms such as `P is true`, `P is the case`, and `P is not false` work for proposition letters, quoted statements, explicitly named propositions, and parenthesized symbolic formulas. These readings work in both front and trailing conditions. Undelimited complex suffix operands are not automatically stripped, because their scope can be unclear.

`Provided (that)`, `providing (that)`, and `supposing (that)` remain supported conditional markers. Reports such as `we suppose P`, `one supposes P`, `P is supposed`, `it is supposed that P`, and the corresponding `take/takes/taken ... to be true/the case` forms are not reduced to P. Supposing or accepting a statement does not entail its truth. Putting such a report after `if` does not make it equivalent to `if P`; use `supposing that P, Q` for the intended hypothetical conditional.

`conditional-truth-r27-test.js` checks truth polarity, both conditional positions, contractions, quoted and parenthesized scope, non-equivalent attitude reports, and the symbolic precedence documented briefly in Help.

The expanded corpus covers necessary and sufficient conditions, biconditionals, conjunctions, inclusive disjunctions, negation, truth wrappers, universal and existential quantification. It is independent of the warrant-claim corpus.

Examples, with P and Q replaced by complete clauses:

* `“P” is a sufficient condition for “Q”`: P implies Q.
* `“P” is a necessary condition for “Q”`: Q implies P.
* `“P” is a necessary and sufficient condition for “Q”`: both directions.
* `On the assumption that P, Q` and `Supposing that P, Q`: conditional constructions.
* `Both “P” and “Q” are true`: conjunction.
* `Both “P” and “Q” are false`: conjunction of denials, not merely denial of their conjunction.
* `P or Q, or both` and `At least one of “P” and “Q” is true`: inclusive disjunction.
* `It isn't so that P`, `The proposition that P is false`, and `“P” is false`: denial of P, preserving the scope of any.
* `It is the case that P` and `It is true that P`: transparent truth wrappers. Knowledge and necessity wrappers are not transparent.
* `Every single one of the ravens is black` and `Each and every one of the ravens is black`: universal quantification.
* `No single raven is white` and `There does not exist any raven that is white`: negative existential / universal denial.

The tests include wrong-direction conditionals, affirming the consequent, exclusive versus inclusive disjunction, exceptions to universal claims, exactly-one versus at-least-one, and modal qualifiers. Unknown constructions are not evidence that an argument is invalid. English scope, temporal readings and word senses still require care.

Classical interpretation references consulted: [OpenStax logical statements](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-2-logical-statements), [OpenStax compound statements](https://openstax.org/books/contemporary-mathematics/pages/2-2-compound-statements), and [Stanford introduction to propositional logic](https://logic.stanford.edu/intrologic/chapters/chapter_02.html).

### Commutation of like quantifiers

Quantifier Commutation exchanges one adjacent pair of universal quantifiers or one adjacent pair of existential quantifiers, including within a larger formula. It preserves the body and its variable bindings. Mixed quantifiers are not interchangeable. This is an established derived equivalence, as described in [Cohen's multiple-quantifier notes](https://faculty.washington.edu/smcohen/120/Chapter11.pdf), not an additional logical axiom.

The checker accepts the direct replacement and a conditional expressing it as a Rule Instance. Quantified subformulas retain their syntax trees, so universal elimination and existential introduction can also be recognized in conditional Rule Instances. Written conditional boundaries are checked before equivalent flattened forms. Standalone tautologies and mere repetition are still not automatically licensed. Automatic parent generation does not propose quantifier rearrangements.

`quantifier-commutation-r27-test.js` checks direct and conditional forms, alpha-renaming, nested replacements, rejection of mixed swaps and changed bindings, and an independent exhaustive check of binary relations on a two-element domain. No assumption/scope nodes are introduced.

The reviewed supplement in `language-data/reviewed-variants.json` adds explicit British/American spellings (including inflected forms), six selected alternate past-tense families from the verb snapshot, and eight compound plurals. Regional spellings match during checking; original box text is not rewritten, and generated conclusions retain the first corresponding spelling found in the premises. Capital-marked names remain distinct. This is a finite list, not a general suffix substitution. In particular, `analyses` normalizes as a verb but remains the plural of `analysis` as a noun. `licence/license` and `metre/meter` are deliberately not globally equated.

Additional qualification tests cover `we aren't sure that P`, `one is not certain that P`, and `we cannot rule out (the possibility) that P` / `it cannot be ruled out that P`. The latter challenges establishment of not-P, not establishment of P. Negation and conjunction inside P retain their scope. Tense/aspect differences and reversal of participants remain distinct; active/passive conversion is not added by this supplement.

Remaining audit work includes sense-sensitive alternate forms, accented nouns outside the present token grammar, and contextual interpretation of bare homographs. The source-import exclusions do not override earlier curated mappings: bare `found`, `fell`, and similar forms are not yet fully disambiguated by subject/context. Explicit `did find` versus `did found` is tested as distinct. These limitations must not be described as complete word-sense coverage.

`logical-locutions-r27.json` is the independently written phrasing corpus. `language-r27-expanded-test.js` checks these phrasings, inference direction, all automatically imported word-form mappings, source participles, and snapshot integrity. Existing deductive, English and randomized stress suites remain in use. See `language-data/README.md` for source snapshots and import exclusions, and `WARRANT-LANGUAGE-r27.md` for argument-warrant constructions.
